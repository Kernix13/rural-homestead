## How to use local county offices to find land

### PVA office

- Search parcel maps and identify vacant land
- Filter by land use codes
- See who owns the land and where they live
- Look for tax mailing addresses different from the property address — a sign of absentee ownership and possible willingness to sell
- 📌 You can write down parcel IDs or owner names and then check those in the deed or tax records

### County Clerk or Deed Office

- Ownership (deeds) — check for recent transfers or heirs
- Probate records — land inherited may be sitting unused
- Foreclosure notices or lis pendens filings
- Liens — owners under pressure may be motivated to sell
- search for: Recent estate transfers or deaths | Properties that haven't sold in 20+ years (possible older owners)

### Tax Office for Delinquent or Surplus Properties - Sheriff's Department

- Delinquent property tax lists — landowners who haven't paid taxes might be open to selling
- Tax lien sales or tax deed auctions
- Counties publish a list of tax-delinquent properties annually or view that list in person or online
- Ask the Tax Office: "Can I see the most recent list of tax-delinquent properties?" | "Are there any surplus properties the county owns or plans to auction?"

> **Combine GIS + County Offices**: Use GIS to identify vacant or `odd-shaped` parcels > Cross-reference the owner using deed records > Check if they're behind on taxes > _Send a letter or postcard asking if they're open to selling_
> "_Hi, I'm looking for land in your area and saw you own a parcel at XYZ. Would you consider selling it_?"

Delinquent taxes in Kentucky: https://revenue.ky.gov/Property/pages/delinquent-property-tax.aspx

1. Beginning in mid-July, county clerks offer the certificates of delinquency for sale to third party purchasers. These sales run through the latter part of October with the majority of sales taking place from mid-July through the end of August

- vacant land means land without a building: 🧭 You'll often see this noted in the land use field as something like "Residential Vacant," "Agricultural," or "Vacant Land (Recreational)."
- Land Use Codes or Property Class Codes are used for tax assessment and classification:

| Land Use Code     | What It Means           | Can You Build a Home There |
| :---------------- | :---------------------- | :------------------------- |
| 100 - Residential | There is already a home | Already developed          |
| 300 - Vacant Land | Likely no structure     | Possibly                   |

- 👉 You can often buy ag land and build a home, but it depends on: County zoning rules, Minimum acreage for home construction, Utility access (power, septic, water)

### Why Look at Owner Info and Mailing Address

- about finding people to contact directly, because many of them:
  - Are not using the land actively
  - Live far away (absentee owners)
  - Might be interested in selling
- If the owner's mailing address is different from the land's location, that's a good candidate

### Sending a letter

two primary letter templates should cover most scenarios when reaching out to landowners:

1. General Inquiry Letter: For properties identified via the PVA or GIS systems.
2. Tax-Delinquent Property Letter: For properties identified through the Tax Office as being delinquent on taxes.

> See in docx: 1. General Inquiry Letter (PVA/GIS Identified Properties) | 2. Tax-Delinquent Property Letter

### Check for recent transfers or heirs

When a property has recently changed hands—especially via inheritance (deed transfer to an heir or through probate)—the new owner may not have a strong emotional tie to the land and may be more open to selling it. These properties are sometimes unlisted and sitting unused.

- The General Inquiry Letter I gave earlier is usually appropriate here

### Probate records / inherited land

Land that's inherited can sit vacant or unused for years—especially if the heir lives out of state or has no personal interest in the property

- similar to vacant land, but your approach may need to be a little more sensitive since it might involve a recent loss
- You can use the same General Inquiry Letter

> See docx for Inherited Property Letter (optional variation)

### Foreclosure notices or lis pendens filings

These indicate legal action involving unpaid debt—often mortgage-related. Property owners in this situation may be open to a quick sale before losing the property. Reaching out in these situations may help them avoid foreclosure.

- These can be sensitive cases. If you write, be respectful and offer a possible solution, not pressure
- You can adapt the _Tax-Delinquent Letter_ to suit foreclosure/lis pendens—similar language applies

### Use the Tax Office for Delinquent or Surplus Properties

When a landowner hasn't paid property taxes for a year or more, it often means:

- They're not using or maintaining the property
- They may be struggling financially
- They've moved away or inherited the land and don't care to keep it

These owners are often very open to selling, especially if you reach out before the county puts the property up for a tax lien sale.

The county tax office usually keeps a delinquent tax list — sometimes available online or in person.

> see docx for Draft Letter: Tax-Delinquent Landowner

### Tax Lien Sales or Tax Deed Auctions

- Tax lien sales: The county sells a lien on the property to investors who pay the back taxes. The owner keeps the property if they pay off the lien with interest. If they don't, the lienholder can eventually get the property.
- Tax deed sales: The county takes ownership after unpaid taxes and sells the property outright
- You may:
  - Buy directly from the auction, often at a discount
  - Contact owners before the auction to make a deal, giving them quick cash and avoiding the county process

> Ask about **upcoming tax sales**, **surplus property**, or **lists of unsold parcels**

## How to View Only the Right Parcels

1. Ask for help turning on useful map layers: Parcel boundaries, Acreage labels, Land use codes / property class overlays
2. Visually screen parcels using: Size (1–5 acre parcels), No visible structures, Location (areas where zoning or proximity to roads makes it possible to build a home), Avoid obvious flood zones or utility easements
3. Click only on parcels that seem promising (in the office): write down Parcel ID or number, Location, Acres
4. Back at home: Use free tools to research further: Use free PVA websites, county tax lookup, or Whitepages/reverse address tools to find Owner names, Mailing addresses

Viewing around 30–75 records is typical. This includes:

- Zooming in on parcels that look promising on the map
- Clicking to view ownership, acreage, land use
- Skipping parcels that are clearly unsuitable (e.g., subdivisions, steep land, industrial)

> **Agricultural land: Land used for farming or pasture, typically lower in cost per acre**
> Often bigger, cheaper, more rural | May require rezoning or special approval to build a home | Could have deed restrictions, soil or access limitations
> **rural land for homesteading, tiny homes, or cabins is often coded as agricultural**

Look at both residential and ag, but prioritize

1. Vacant residential lots first (simpler, fewer zoning hurdles)
2. Undeveloped ag parcels 1–5 acres second — but check with Planning & Zoning before pursuing

**When to avoid a parcel**: If the zoning prohibits housing outright

Questions to Ask:

> 1. "Can I get a list or spreadsheet of parcels between 1 and 5 acres that are vacant or coded as agricultural?"
> 2. "Can you filter for properties with out-of-county or absentee owners?"
> 3. "Is there a public access terminal where I can screen parcels for free?"
> 4. "Can I take photos or screenshots of the parcel IDs and then research them later from home?"

- 100 – Residential: May include improved (with a house) or vacant
- 101 – Vacant Residential: Ideal for homes or cabins
- 500 – Farm/Agricultural: Check zoning or planning office before buying
- 501 – Vacant Ag Land: Look for size, access, utilities, buildability

Zoning

- Residential (R-1, R-2): Smaller lots, may be in subdivisions
- Agricultural (A-1, AG): May need special permits or rezoning
- Rural Residential: Often perfect for 1–5 acres, Check setback rules and utilities

> 💡 Tip: Always confirm zoning and permitted uses with the County Planning & Zoning office, not just the PVA.

📌 Quick Questions to Ask at the PVA or Planning Office

- "What zoning allows for single-family homes or cabins?"
- "Can you help me identify parcels with no structures?"
- "What's the minimum lot size for building in unincorporated areas?"
- "Is a site evaluation or soil test required to build?"

## Utility Easements

These are harder to spot unless the PVA map has a utility or right-of-way layer

- If the parcel borders power lines, pipelines, or major roads, it may have an easement
- Ask the PVA or County Clerk: "Does your GIS system show easements or rights of way on the map?"
- You can also search the deed (at the County Clerk's office) — easements are often recorded there

Viewing utility easements is important because they directly affect what you can and cannot do with the land — especially if you want to build a home, cabin, or driveway

A utility easement gives utility companies (like electric, water, gas, or cable) the legal right to access part of a property to install or maintain infrastructure

- You might not be able to build on that section of the land
- You cannot place structures, fences, or even trees in the easement area
- The utility company can enter your land at any time (without asking).
- They can dig, cut down trees, and access their equipment as needed
- You won't be compensated for damage caused during maintenance
- On a small parcel, a large easement could take up valuable buildable land

✅ How to Check:

- Ask the PVA or County Clerk if easements are shown on their GIS or recorded in the deed
- Look for labels like "R/W" (right-of-way) or "easement" lines on the GIS map
- Consider having a surveyor review the parcel before you buy, especially if it's a good price — sometimes the price reflects an easement issue.
